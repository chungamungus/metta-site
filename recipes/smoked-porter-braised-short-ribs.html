Smoked Porter Braised Short Ribs

Smoked potato and leek purée, roasted carrots, gremolata

← All recipes

This is the braise that brewing gave me. A smoked porter does what red wine does in a classic braise, adding acidity and depth, but it also brings roasted malt and a whisper of campfire that makes the whole pot taste like it spent the afternoon over coals. It's my most requested winter main, and it's better on day two, which makes it perfect for a dinner party.

The one thing to watch: the roasted malt in a porter reads as bitterness if you reduce the sauce too hard. A spoonful of brown sugar and a splash of vinegar at the end keep it in balance.

  • Serves6
  • Active time45 minutes
  • Total time4 hours, plus overnight salting

A cold-weather main. I cook this from the first frost through mud season.

Ingredients

Short ribs

  • 5 lb bone-in English-cut beef short ribs (about 8 pieces)
  • Kosher salt and black pepper
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil
  • 2 yellow onions, roughly chopped
  • 2 carrots, roughly chopped
  • 2 celery stalks, roughly chopped
  • 6 garlic cloves, smashed
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 2 tablespoons all-purpose flour
  • 2 (12 oz) bottles smoked porter
  • 2 cups beef or chicken stock
  • 2 bay leaves and 5 sprigs thyme
  • 1 tablespoon dark brown sugar
  • 1 tablespoon apple cider vinegar

Gremolata

  • 1/2 cup flat-leaf parsley, finely chopped
  • Zest of 1 lemon
  • 1 small garlic clove, grated
  • Pinch of flaky salt

Method

  1. The night before, season the ribs generously with salt on all sides and refrigerate uncovered on a rack. This seasons the meat through and dries the surface for a better sear.
  2. Heat the oven to 300°F. Pat the ribs dry and season with pepper. Heat the oil in a large Dutch oven over medium-high and brown the ribs in batches, 3 to 4 minutes per side, until deeply colored. Don't crowd the pot. Set the ribs aside.
  3. Pour off all but 2 tablespoons of fat. Add the onions, carrots, celery, and garlic with a pinch of salt and cook until softened and starting to brown, about 8 minutes. Stir in the tomato paste and cook 2 minutes, then sprinkle in the flour and stir for 1 minute.
  4. Pour in the porter, scraping up everything stuck to the bottom. Bring to a simmer and let it reduce by about a third, 5 minutes. Add the stock, bay, and thyme.
  5. Nestle the ribs back in, bone side down. The liquid should come about two-thirds of the way up. Cover, transfer to the oven, and braise until a fork slides in with no resistance, 3 to 3 1/2 hours.
  6. Carefully lift the ribs out. Strain the braising liquid into a saucepan, pressing on the vegetables, and skim the fat (or chill overnight and lift it off in one piece, which is what I do). Simmer to reduce until it coats a spoon, then stir in the brown sugar and vinegar. Taste for salt.
  7. Mix the gremolata just before serving. Reheat the ribs gently in the sauce, then plate over the purée with the carrots alongside and a spoonful of gremolata on top.

Chef’s notes

  • Beer: Stone Smoked Porter and Alaskan Smoked Porter are the classics. A regular porter or a stout works too; you'll lose the campfire note but keep the depth.
  • Smoked potato and leek purée: boil 2 lb Yukon Golds, sweat 2 sliced leeks in butter, blend both with 1 cup warm cream and a pinch of smoked salt or a drop of liquid smoke. Pass through a sieve if you want it restaurant-smooth.
  • Make it a day ahead. The flavor improves and the fat is easy to remove once cold.
